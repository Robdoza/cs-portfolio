# Sprint Review and Retrospective – SNHU Travel Project

## Project Overview
This artifact demonstrates my application of Scrum-Agile practices during a simulated client project for SNHU Travel. The project involved iterative development, stakeholder feedback, and structured sprint events.

## Reflection

### Interpreting User Needs
At its core, user needs interpretation starts with listening. For example, stakeholder feedback directly inspired features like the “Top Five Destination List” during SNHU Travel. Instead of framing this as merely a visual element, the requirement was decomposed through user-centered stories that determined who the feature was for and what its importance was.

Writing user stories in “As a user, I want to…, so that…” format helped keep development choices grounded to actual value rather than technical tasks. Rather than building everything all at once, the feature was built out incrementally. First, it had a list structure, then descriptions, then images, then embedded links. This approach of incremental delivery ensured we had full working functionality at each step along the journey as well defined acceptance criteria.

User stories would eliminate the ambiguity, make the intention clear and align development with what matters to stakeholders. They act as a liaison between human requirements and technical realization.

### Approach to Development
To me development is an iterative, feedback-oriented process rather than a one-off implementation exercise. The SNHU Travel project proved to us the value of a build small, test often and adapt quickly when requirements change approach.

Sprint planning, backlog prioritization, daily progress check-ins, sprint reviews and retrospectives kept focus and visibility through agile practices. Principles of inspection and adaptation were particularly useful. Technical issues, either related to image handling or JAR packaging, for example were handled in short sprint cycles instead of growing into huge downstream concerns.

Now in future work, I will add structure to this process with structured sprints, backlog and planning & retrospect. Even in solo projects, keeping a backlog, writing acceptance criteria and reflecting on possible process improvement can help improve both efficiency and product quality. In contrast to fixed assumptions, Agile welcomes change and thus minimizes risk.

### Being a Good Team Member
A good teammate contributes not just technically, but collaboratively. This was a great learning experience of understanding the value of accountability, communication and shared ownership through rotations between Product Owner, Scrum Master and Development Team member roles.

A good team member raises blockers early, honors clearly defined roles and works toward the common goal of a sprint rather than for individual glory. Data-driven decision making is used to minimize redundancy by working from a single source of truth and clearly agreed acceptance criteria, as well as structured communication during sprint events.

Good teamwork in software development requires transparency, adaptability, and respect for collective success. Software projects are rarely the result of isolated effort. They succeed when team members align around a shared vision and actively support one another in delivering value.
